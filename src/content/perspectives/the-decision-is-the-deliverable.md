The [previous piece](/perspectives/what-product-management-is-not) was a
subtraction. It argued that most product careers accumulate adjacent skills —
backlog craft, stakeholder translation, launch choreography, dashboard
maintenance — because those skills are *legible* and judgement isn't, and that the
market is about to stop paying for the legible ones.

Subtraction is the easy half. The fair question is the one that arrives about
thirty seconds later: **fine, then what am I supposed to be doing on Monday?**

That question deserves better than the usual answer. "Focus on outcomes, not
output" is true, has been true for fifteen years, and changes nobody's calendar —
for the same reason "involve product marketing earlier" changes nobody's
staffing. Advice that ignores why the current behaviour is rational is just a
politer description of something that isn't going to happen.

So this piece has two jobs. Name the craft precisely enough to practise it. Then
solve the legibility problem, because a discipline that produces no artefacts
cannot be rewarded, cannot be taught, and cannot be defended in a reorg.

## The definition, and the four crafts hiding inside it

The last piece ended on a definition, so start there and take it apart:

> Product management is the practice of **deciding what is worth building**, on
> **incomplete evidence**, in a **market that won't hold still** — and being
> **accountable for the answer**.

Four clauses. Each one is a distinct craft with its own technique, its own
evidence standard, and its own way of going wrong. Together they're the whole
job; anything that doesn't serve one of them is overhead you've agreed to carry.

| The clause | The craft | What good looks like | The adjacent failure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| What is *worth* building | **Problem selection** | Choosing problems that are funded, painful, and where you have unfair leverage | Choosing problems that are interesting, or that were asked for loudest |
| On incomplete evidence | **Evidence under time pressure** | Spending the least evidence that resolves the decision, matched to reversal cost | Research as reassurance; or shipping as a way to avoid finding out |
| A market that won't hold still | **Re-deciding** | Decisions carry expiry dates and get revisited on schedule | Treating a past decision as settled because it was once correct |
| Accountable for the answer | **Public accountability** | Predictions written down, calls owned by name, wrongness on record | Diffuse ownership; "the data told us"; retroactive narrative |

Notice what's *not* in the table. Not writing, not facilitation, not technical
depth, not domain knowledge, not communication. Those are all real and I'd take
them in any PM I hired — but they're **multipliers**, not the thing being
multiplied. A brilliant communicator with no problem-selection craft communicates
the wrong roadmap very persuasively, which is worse than doing it badly. This is
the single most common way strong people end up in the counterfeit trap: they get
rewarded for the multiplier until it becomes the identity.

## Craft one: problem selection is the whole game

If you only fix one thing, fix this, because everything downstream inherits its
quality. A team executing beautifully against a badly chosen problem is a more
expensive failure than a team executing sloppily against a good one.

Problem selection has three filters, and a problem needs all three:

**Is the pain funded?** Not "is it real" — real pain is abundant and mostly
unmonetisable. Is there a budget line, or a person whose bonus depends on it?
This is the filter that kills the most beloved ideas, and it's why
[willingness to pay](/marginalia/willingness-to-pay) is a discovery input rather
than a pricing-committee input. Asking it in month one is cheap. Asking it after
two quarters of build is a layoff conversation.

**What is the customer doing today instead?** Every problem worth solving is
already being solved badly — by a spreadsheet, an intern, a competitor, or
nothing. That's the [competitive alternative](/marginalia/competitive-alternative),
and the honest version includes *doing nothing*, which is the market leader in
most categories. If you can't describe the current workaround in specific detail,
you haven't selected a problem; you've selected a topic.

**Where do we have unfair leverage?** Distribution you already own, data nobody
else has, a right to be believed in this category, a structural cost advantage.
A problem you can solve exactly as well as four competitors is not a strategy,
it's a [Red Queen race](/marginalia/red-queen-effect) — you run to stay in place.
Leverage is what converts a good problem into *your* good problem, and it's the
part [positioning](/marginalia/positioning) work exists to find.

The output of this craft is not a roadmap. It's a short, defended, written claim:
*this problem, for this segment, because of this leverage, and here's what we're
consciously not solving.* If your organisation runs [PR-FAQs](/marginalia/pr-faq),
this is what the document is actually for — not the announcement, but forcing the
claim into the open early enough to be argued with.

## Craft two: spend the least evidence that resolves the decision

The counterfeit version of evidence is volume — more interviews, more analysis,
a bigger deck. The craft version is an efficiency question: **what is the cheapest
observation that would change my mind, and can I get it this week?**

Two ideas do most of the work here.

**Match evidence spend to reversal cost, not to how important it feels.** Amazon's
one-way/two-way door distinction is the cleanest formulation: decisions that are
cheap to reverse should be made fast and with thin evidence, because the cost of
deliberation exceeds the cost of being wrong. Decisions that are expensive to
reverse — an architecture, a pricing model, a category you'll be filed under —
deserve genuine evidence spend. Most teams get this exactly backwards: weeks of
debate on a reversible UI choice, and a pricing model chosen in one meeting
because it felt like a detail.

**Climb the evidence ladder from the bottom.** Each rung costs roughly ten times
the one below it, and the discipline is refusing to skip rungs:

1. Evidence you already have — support tickets, churn interviews, usage data,
   the sales team's loss reasons. Nearly free, routinely unread.
2. Five conversations with people who have the problem. Days, not weeks.
3. A concierge or manual version — you deliver the outcome by hand for three
   customers. Fastest way to discover the problem isn't what you thought.
4. A fake door or landing page — measures intent rather than opinion.
5. A prototype in front of ten real users.
6. Build it.

Almost every team I've watched jumps from rung 1 to rung 6, and calls rung 6 the
learning. Building is a legitimate way to learn, but it is the most expensive one
available, and choosing it first is usually not confidence — it's an unwillingness
to be told no before the resources are committed.

The failure mode on this craft is subtler than laziness: **research as
reassurance.** Interviews commissioned to settle an internal argument, framed so
the answer is safe, read for confirmation. The test is whether you wrote down,
before collecting anything, what result would make you abandon the idea. If you
didn't, you weren't gathering evidence. You were gathering support.

## Craft three: decisions expire, so schedule the re-decide

This is the craft nobody teaches and everyone needs, because it's where good
product organisations rot slowly enough that nobody notices.

[Product-market fit decays](/marginalia/pmf-decay). A decision was a match between
a belief and a market condition, and market conditions expire — competitors move,
regulation lands, a platform shift changes what's cheap, the segment you won
matures. A decision that was correct in 2024 and is still in force in 2026 isn't
a strategy; it's sediment. Yet almost no organisation has a mechanism for
revisiting a settled call, because reopening a decision reads socially as
disloyalty to the person who made it.

The fix is mechanical, not cultural: **every significant decision gets an expiry
date and a named condition that would trigger an early review.** "We revisit this
in two quarters, or immediately if enterprise deal cycles stretch past 90 days."
Written at the time of the decision, when nobody is defending anything yet, the
review is pre-authorised — it happens because it was scheduled, not because
someone was brave.

Two things this buys you beyond freshness. It converts strategy from a document
into a cadence, which is the only form it survives in. And it makes changing your
mind low-status-cost, which is a precondition for anyone telling you the truth
early.

## Craft four: be wrong in public, on purpose

The last clause of the definition is the one that makes the other three real. An
unaccountable decision isn't a decision; it's a preference with a budget.

Accountability here has a specific technical meaning, and it isn't "taking the
blame." Annie Duke's term for the confusion is *resulting* — grading a decision
by its outcome rather than by its quality at the time it was made. Products are
high-variance; good calls lose and bad calls win often enough that outcome-grading
teaches you almost nothing, and worse, teaches the organisation to punish
sensible risk. What you grade instead is the **process and the calibration**: what
did you believe, how confident were you, what would have changed your mind, and
how did that hold up?

Which requires the thing almost nobody does: writing the prediction down, with a
number, before the outcome. It feels exposing the first three times and then
becomes the most valuable professional habit available to you, because it's the
only way to discover that your confidence is systematically miscalibrated in one
direction — and it always is, in one direction, and you can't feel which.

The companion habit is the **no-list**: a running record of what you declined,
why, and what it would have cost. Every serious product organisation runs on a
functioning [institutional "no"](/marginalia/institutional-no), and the individual
version is what stops your roadmap being an inbox. It also happens to be the
single most persuasive artefact in a performance review, which brings us to the
part that actually determines whether any of this survives.

## Making judgement legible: the decision record

Here is the crux. The previous piece argued that people build counterfeit skills
because those skills are countable at quarter-end while judgement produces
nothing to point at. If that diagnosis is right, then "develop better judgement"
is useless advice on its own. **The fix is to give judgement an artefact.**

That artefact is a decision record — one page, written at the moment of the call,
not after. It is not a PRD, it does not go through review, and it exists whether
or not your organisation asks for one.

| Field | What goes in it | Why it earns its place |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The question** | The decision phrased as a question, dated | Forces the actual decision into the open; half of them dissolve here |
| **Options, including "do nothing"** | Two or three real ones | If there was only ever one option, this was execution, not a decision |
| **The load-bearing belief** | The single assumption that, if false, changes the answer | Names the thing to test, instead of testing everything |
| **Confidence** | A number, honestly | The only field that makes calibration measurable later |
| **What would change my mind** | Named in advance, specifically | The anti-rationalisation clause; without it the record is a press release |
| **The call, and whose it is** | One name | Diffuse ownership is how decisions escape review |
| **Reversal cost** | Cheap / expensive, and why | Sets how much evidence this deserves — see craft two |
| **Expires** | A date, plus an early-trigger condition | The re-decide, pre-authorised — see craft three |

Ten minutes each. Twenty of them a year is a document set that no amount of ticket
throughput can compete with, and it does something no other artefact does: it
makes the invisible part of your work **countable, reviewable, and teachable.**
It is also, incidentally, the training data for your own judgement — the only way
to find out which kinds of calls you're good at.

## Where the time comes from

None of this is additive. The calendar is already full, and it's full of the
counterfeits, so the honest version of this advice is a trade:

| Stop spending the marginal hour on | Spend it on |
| --- | --- |
| Refining tickets two sprints out | The decision record for the quarter's biggest unknown |
| Status reporting upward | A prediction, with a date and a number |
| Gathering requirements as a service | Recovering the problem underneath, and maintaining the no-list |
| Attending ceremonies for coverage | Five customer conversations a week, protected like a ceremony |
| Building the fuller dashboard | Choosing the one number, and naming what would make you abandon it |
| Launch choreography | Week-two and week-twelve accountability for what you launched |

The last row is the hardest and the most diagnostic. Nobody's calendar is booked
for week twelve. Which is precisely why almost nobody knows whether their launches
worked — and why [time to first value](/marginalia/time-to-first-value) is worth
more attention than launch-day traffic ever repays.

## How this survives a performance review

The organisation still grades activity. That doesn't change because you read an
essay, so the practical skill is translating judgement into the register your
organisation already counts. Three moves, all of which work:

**Claim cancellations as savings, with arithmetic.** Not "we decided not to build
the integrations hub." Instead: *"Killed it in month two on evidence — three of
the five design partners were already served by a partner connector. Redeployed
two engineers for three quarters."* That is a countable contribution, and it is
almost always the largest one you made that year. Most PMs never say it out loud
because cancellation feels like absence of work rather than the product of it.

**Report calibration, not just results.** "Eleven predictions logged, seven
resolved, five correct — I'm systematically overconfident on adoption timing and
have started halving my estimates." No manager has heard this before. It reads as
seniority because it is.

**Show the no-list with a value attached.** A list of what you declined and what
it would have consumed is the clearest possible evidence that a decision function
exists in your team and is being exercised. In an organisation without one, it is
also a quiet argument for why you should be given more scope.

In interviews the same material is a step-change, because every candidate can
describe something they shipped and almost none can describe something they
killed, why, and what they got wrong along the way. The negative-space piece
offered the cancellation question as a diagnostic for hiring managers. This is the
answer to it — and the reason to start now is that the answer can only be built
in retrospect.

## The AI corollary, running the other way

The last piece made the pessimistic version of the argument: the counterfeits map
almost exactly onto what models already do well, and they got to be the job
because coordinating scarce build capacity used to be expensive. Here's the
optimistic half.

When production and coordination collapse in cost, **the constraint moves to
problem selection and to killing things** — the two crafts with the least
automation exposure, because both are fundamentally acts of refusal made under
accountability. Agents can generate ten plausible roadmaps; they cannot want a
particular outcome, carry the consequence of a bad call, or hold a position under
commercial pressure. The scarce input becomes a well-posed problem and a person
willing to own the answer, which is a *larger* job than the one being displaced,
not a smaller one.

Two second-order effects, both underrated. Cheap building lowers the cost of every
rung on the evidence ladder — concierge versions, fake doors and prototypes that
used to cost a sprint now cost an afternoon, so the excuse for skipping straight
to "build it and see" is gone. And the risk profile shifts: when the machine
produces confident, well-formed, *plausible* answers, the dominant failure is the
[plausible-versus-correct gap](/marginalia/plausible-versus-correct-gap), and the
work becomes [trust calibration](/marginalia/trust-calibration) — knowing which
outputs you're allowed to act on and which need a human's name against them.
That's not a new discipline; it's craft two and craft four, applied to a new
source of evidence.

The upstream seats that were previously unaffordable open up at the same time —
the [synthetic product quad](/marginalia/synthetic-product-quad) argument. The
job that's left is smaller in hours and much larger in consequence.

## Ninety days, if you scored badly on the audit

For anyone who ran the five tests in the last piece and didn't like the result.
None of this needs permission.

- **Weeks 1–2.** Start the decision record. Backfill the last two significant
  decisions from memory — including confidence as you actually felt it, not as
  you'd like to have felt it. Notice how many turn out not to have been decisions.
- **Weeks 3–4.** Write one prediction with a date and a number, and tell one
  person. Start the no-list. Move one recurring ceremony out of the calendar and
  put two customer conversations in its place.
- **Month 2.** Kill something. A small something is fine — the skill being built
  is doing it on stated evidence and saying so publicly, not the size of the
  saving. Add an expiry date to the biggest decision currently in force on your
  team.
- **Month 3.** Take the largest open unknown and climb the evidence ladder from
  rung one deliberately, stopping the moment the decision resolves. Review the
  first predictions. Write the calibration line for your next review.

At the end you will not have transformed. You'll have four or five decision
records, a short no-list, and one uncomfortable data point about your own
overconfidence — which is roughly one year's worth of judgement compressed into a
quarter, and more than most people accumulate in five.

## The receipt and the work

The counterfeits are all artefacts: the ticket, the spec, the launch plan, the
dashboard. That's why they're seductive, and it's why they're the first things to
be automated — an artefact is a description of work, and descriptions are exactly
what these machines produce.

The work itself was always the decision. What's worth building, on evidence you
wish were better, in a market that has already started moving, with your name on
it. The document is just the receipt.

Write more receipts.
