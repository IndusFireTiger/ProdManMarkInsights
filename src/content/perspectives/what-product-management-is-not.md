Here is a CV I have read many versions of. Ten years in product. Forty-plus
features shipped. Twelve launches. A backlog kept so clean it could be audited.
Stakeholders who describe the person as *responsive* and *unflappable*. Two
certifications. Promoted twice.

And here is the question that empties the room: **what did you decide not to
build, and what evidence killed it?**

Usually there is no answer. Not because the person is a fraud — they have worked
extremely hard, in an actual job, at things their organisation genuinely wanted —
but because the ten years were spent accumulating a *different* skill from the one
on the label. The tragedy of product management is not that people do it badly.
It's that a large share of practitioners spend a decade getting excellent at
adjacent disciplines, are rewarded the whole time, and arrive at senior level
without the one capability the title is supposed to certify.

So this is a piece about the negative space. Not what product management is —
there is no shortage of writing on that — but the things it is *reliably mistaken
for*, why the mistake is rational rather than stupid, and what it costs you when
you're twelve years in and the market changes underneath the skill you actually
built.

## Experience is decisions, not duration

Start with the measurement error, because everything downstream follows from it.

We price product experience in years. Years are a proxy for *exposure*, and
exposure is a proxy for *judgement*. Both proxies leak. What actually builds
product judgement is a specific and surprisingly rare event: **you make a
consequential call under real uncertainty, you commit resources to it, and you
stay long enough to see whether you were right.** That's one unit of experience.
Everything else is attendance.

Run the count honestly and most PM careers produce fewer of these than you'd
expect — perhaps a handful a year in a healthy setup, and in some organisations
zero, for years, because the consequential calls were made above the role and
arrived as a roadmap. You can log a decade of full weeks and hold a single-digit
number of calibrated decisions. That is what "ten years of the wrong experience"
means. It isn't laziness. It's a career spent downstream of the decision.

The counterfeits below all share one structural property: **they are legible.**
They produce artefacts you can point at in a performance review — tickets,
specs, launches, dashboards, satisfied stakeholders. Judgement produces almost no
artefacts, and its feedback arrives eighteen months late, by which time the
attribution is unrecoverable. Given a choice between a skill that is graded
quarterly and a skill that is graded never, people rationally build the first
one. That's the whole mechanism.

## The eight counterfeits

| The mistaken identity | The discipline it actually belongs to | What you accumulate | What you never learn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CEO of the product | Executive authority | Meeting-craft, escalation paths, org politics | How to move a decision you can't order |
| Owner of the backlog | Delivery / agile management | Refinement, estimation, throughput | What deserved building at all |
| Voice of the stakeholders | Business analysis | Elicitation, documentation fidelity | How to refuse a request with evidence |
| The ideas person | Creative direction | Ideation volume, framework fluency | How to kill your own good idea |
| The domain expert | Subject-matter expertise | Depth in one context | Whether the context still holds |
| The technical authority | Engineering / architecture | Solution design, technical credibility | Where technical depth actually pays |
| The launch | Programme management / comms | Choreography, readiness checklists | What happened after week two |
| The dashboard | Analytics | Instrumentation, reporting cadence | Which number is lying to you |

Every row is a real, valuable, employable skill. Several of them are skills a
strong PM needs *some* of. The failure is substitution — building the adjacent
skill until it fills the space where the job should be, and mistaking the filled
calendar for a developing career.

Four of these cost more than the others. They're worth taking one at a time.

## Not authority: the CEO myth teaches you to practise the wrong thing

"The product manager is the CEO of the product" comes from Ben Horowitz's
Netscape-era memo, and it was making a narrow point about ownership and
accountability. Read literally three decades later, it has become the most
expensive sentence in the discipline — because it tells a junior PM that the
thing standing between them and effectiveness is **insufficient authority.**

Chase that belief and you spend years practising a specific set of moves:
securing decision rights, widening scope, controlling the roadmap artefact,
managing up, being in the room. None of it is worthless. All of it is
substitutable — those are general corporate skills, and the market pays for them
in a dozen other roles.

The scarce skill is the opposite one. A product manager typically has
accountability without command, and the resulting job is **changing what other
people conclude, using evidence they can check.** That is a craft with real
technique: knowing which evidence a sceptical engineer will accept versus what
convinces a CFO, building the smallest experiment that settles a disagreement,
framing a trade-off so the decision belongs to the room rather than to you. PMs
who solve their authority problem by acquiring authority never develop it. Then
they change companies, arrive without the accumulated political capital, and
discover the skill didn't travel.

The tell: ask someone how they got a contested decision made. If the answer is a
sequence of escalations, they have been practising politics. If the answer is a
piece of evidence and the reason it landed, they have been practising the job.

## Not the backlog: five years of excellent delivery management

This is the most common counterfeit and the one with the friendliest disguise,
because the artefacts are indistinguishable from the real thing. A groomed
backlog, well-written tickets, a predictable sprint, a burndown that behaves. The
team is happy. Delivery is smooth. Every metric on the operating dashboard is
green.

That is a [feature factory](/marginalia/feature-factory), and its defining
property is that **nothing in the operating metrics registers a problem.** The
mature version is orderly and busy. Sprints close, stakeholders are consulted,
releases are celebrated — and the question of whether any of it changed a
customer outcome is owned by nobody in particular.

At the individual level, what you accumulate is genuine expertise in *flow
management*: decomposition, estimation, dependency choreography, keeping a team
unblocked. That's a delivery lead's craft, and a good one is worth a lot. It is
simply not the same craft as deciding what is worth a team-quarter, and five
years of it does not convert.

The diagnostic is the cancellation test, and it is brutal in its simplicity:
**over the last two quarters, what did you stop, and what evidence stopped it?**
A roadmap with no cancellations is not a sign of good picking. It is a sign that
nothing was ever really being decided — that the input queue and the output queue
were the same list, and your role was throughput.

Two things make this counterfeit worse than it used to be. The obvious one is the
title collapse between *product owner* — an agile-ceremony role scoped to a
delivery team — and *product manager*, a market-facing role scoped to an outcome.
The subtler one is that backlog administration is precisely the part of the job
that gets cheaper every quarter. More on that below.

## Not translation: the requirements pipe

The third counterfeit looks like service and is rewarded like virtue. The PM as
conduit: gather what stakeholders want, translate it into something engineering
can build, keep everyone informed, keep everyone happy. Responsiveness is the
performance metric. The artefact is a document with high fidelity to what people
asked for.

The skill you build here is elicitation and documentation — business analysis,
which is a real profession with real rigour, and which most PMs practising it
accidentally are doing at about a 6/10. What you never build is the capability
that makes the role load-bearing: **synthesis, and the refusal that follows from
it.**

Requests are not requirements. A stakeholder request is a solution proposal
carrying an unstated problem, an unstated priority, and an implicit claim that
their context generalises. The job is to recover the problem, weigh it against
every other problem, and then — most of the time — say no, in a way that survives
contact with the person you said it to. Every serious product organisation runs
on a functioning [institutional "no"](/marginalia/institutional-no). A PM who has
never issued one has been operating as middleware.

The related blind spot is where demand comes from. Being fluent in what
stakeholders *say* is not the same as knowing the buyer's
[competitive alternative](/marginalia/competitive-alternative) — including the
spreadsheet they'd happily keep using if you never shipped. Internal fluency
feels like market knowledge and is not; it's the one substitution that's hardest
to detect from inside a building where everyone agrees with you.

## Not accumulation: when tenure starts impersonating expertise

The last of the expensive four is the one that catches *good* people, late, and
it is the reason a decade in one domain can be worth less than four years across
three.

Domain expertise is a genuine asset. It compounds — right up to the point where
it starts substituting for contact with the market. The failure has a shape: you
know the domain so well that customer conversations become confirmation rituals,
research gets commissioned to settle internal arguments rather than to find out
something, and the sentence "I know this market" quietly replaces the evidence it
used to summarise.

Two well-documented errors do most of the damage here.
[Selection bias](/marginalia/selection-bias) — your intuition is trained
overwhelmingly on the customers who bought, stayed, and engaged, which is a
systematically unrepresentative slice of the market you're trying to grow into.
And [survivorship bias](/marginalia/survivorship-bias) — the patterns you learned
from what worked, with no visibility into the identical bets that failed, so the
lesson you extracted may be noise wearing a narrative.

Then there's the moving target underneath.
[Product-market fit decays](/marginalia/pmf-decay): the fit you understood was a
match between a product and a market condition, and market conditions expire.
Expertise built on a snapshot ages into confident wrongness — the most expensive
state a senior product person can occupy, because seniority is exactly what stops
anyone in the room from checking it.

The test here is the transfer test: **how much of your judgement would survive a
move to a different company in a different domain?** Whatever survives is
product skill. Whatever doesn't was domain knowledge, and domain knowledge is
rented, not owned.

## The two quicker ones: the launch and the dashboard

**The launch as finish line.** A launch is a moment of maximum artefact
production — dates, readiness checklists, cross-functional coordination,
visible competence, a Slack channel that celebrates. It is also, structurally,
the point at which you learn the least, because nothing has been used yet. An
organisation that measures product people on launches is running a
[launch factory](/marginalia/launch-factory), and the individual version is a CV
counted in launches rather than in outcomes that held past week two. The honest
question about any shipped thing is what its adoption curve did after the
announcement traffic decayed, and whether anyone was still accountable by then.

**The dashboard as judgement.** Instrumentation is table stakes and measurement
literacy is genuinely part of the craft — but building measurement is not the
same as exercising judgement, and it is frequently a sophisticated way to avoid
it. Scoring frameworks in particular launder taste as arithmetic: a RICE score
is a set of guesses multiplied together, and the multiplication doesn't reduce
the uncertainty in the guesses; it hides it behind a number with decimal places.
What compounds is knowing which metric is a proxy, how it will be gamed once it
becomes a target, and what you'd have to see to conclude you were wrong. What
doesn't compound is owning the report.

## Why the organisation pays for the counterfeits

None of this persists because people don't know better. Every point above is on
the reading list, and has been for years. It persists because the incentives are
real and pull the other way, and any advice that ignores that is just a nicer way
of describing something that isn't going to happen.

Three forces do the work:

1. **Legibility beats value in performance review.** Tickets, launches, and
   dashboards are countable at quarter-end. "Killed a project in month two on
   evidence, freeing a team for eighteen months" is a sentence with no artefact
   attached and a defensive posture built in.
2. **Feedback loops outlast tenure.** Whether a bet paid off surfaces in 12–24
   months. Median PM tenure in a role is often shorter than that. The system
   therefore cannot grade judgement even in principle, so it grades activity —
   and people optimise for the grade that exists.
3. **The counterfeits are what someone is asking for right now.** Every one of
   them started as a genuine request from a stakeholder in genuine need. Saying
   yes to all of them is locally kind, immediately rewarded, and how a decade
   disappears.

Which means the correction is not "try harder." It's to deliberately spend some
of your legibility budget on things that don't score: writing down predictions
before decisions, keeping a record of calls you got wrong, cancelling something
in public.

## The AI turn makes this urgent rather than philosophical

Until recently the counterfeits were survivable. You could build a career on
backlog craft and stakeholder management, and retire on it. That window is
closing, and it's closing precisely along the counterfeit line.

Look at the list again and notice what it has in common: ticket writing, spec
drafting, requirements translation, competitive grids, launch copy, dashboard
assembly, meeting synthesis, status reporting. **This is a near-exact inventory
of what language models already do at acceptable quality, and it is not a
coincidence** — these tasks became the job *because* they were the expensive part
of coordinating scarce build capacity. When the coordination and production cost
collapses, the roles built on top of that cost collapse with it. That's the
[operating-model inversion](/perspectives/ai-product-operating-model): the legacy
product operating model was organised around scarcity, and the scarcity is gone.

The uncomfortable corollary is that cheap building makes a feature factory
*faster*, not smarter. Lowering the cost of production raises the value of
judgement about what deserves producing — and raises the cost of not having any.
Meanwhile the same forces are opening the upstream seats that were previously
unaffordable, which is the argument for a
[synthetic product quad](/marginalia/synthetic-product-quad): market context,
distribution and pricing represented in discovery, not interpreted afterwards.

What survives is short and awkward: deciding what is worth building under real
uncertainty; killing things; setting a position you'll defend when it costs you
something; recovering the actual problem from the request; owning the outcome
when it lands. Notice these are all *judgement under accountability* — the only
part of the job that requires a human to be wrong in public and learn from it.

## Auditing your own experience: five tests

Run these on your last two years, honestly. They take an afternoon and they're
more diagnostic than any competency framework.

1. **The cancellation test.** What did you stop, and what evidence stopped it? No
   cancellations means no decisions.
2. **The wrong-call test.** Name a call you got wrong, when you knew, and what
   changed in how you decide. If nothing comes to mind, you either haven't made
   calls or haven't stayed for the results — both are the same gap.
3. **The counterfactual test.** For your last significant shipped thing: what
   would have happened if you had done nothing? If you can't answer, you have
   shipped without a hypothesis.
4. **The transfer test.** How much of your judgement survives a change of company
   and domain? The remainder is your actual product skill; everything else was
   context you were renting.
5. **The artefact test.** Take your proudest three artefacts from last year. For
   each, ask whether a competent model with your context could produce a
   defensible version this afternoon. Whatever fails this test is where your
   remaining decade of value lives.

Most people score badly on first run. That's the point — the tests are aimed at
the measurement error, not at you, and the fix is a change of where the effort
goes rather than more of it.

## What's left when you subtract all of it

Take away authority, the backlog, the requirements, the ideas, the domain trivia,
the architecture diagrams, the launch, and the dashboard, and something small and
demanding is left standing.

Product management is the practice of **deciding what is worth building, on
incomplete evidence, in a market that won't hold still — and being accountable
for the answer.** Everything else in the job description is a means to that,
useful in proportion to how much it sharpens the decision, and a trap the moment
it becomes the point.

The reason to care now, rather than at some comfortable future review, is that the
counterfeits used to be a slow leak and have become a cliff. The skills that felt
like the job for twenty years are the ones being priced at zero, and the one that
never had an artefact attached is the only one that's appreciating. Ten years is
long enough to become excellent at something. It's worth checking what.
