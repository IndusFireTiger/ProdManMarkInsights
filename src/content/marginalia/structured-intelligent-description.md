Most organisations hold their product knowledge in prose scattered across decks,
wikis, release notes and people's heads. It's legible to colleagues who already
have context and close to useless as an input to anything automated.

A structured intelligent description makes that knowledge addressable: what the
product does, which capability serves which value proposition, which buyer each
one is for, what the competitive alternative is, how it's priced, what stage of
the lifecycle it's in — expressed as data rather than narrative.

The reason it matters is unglamorous. It's the precondition for every downstream
AI play in go-to-market. Agents drafting positioning, localising narratives or
generating tiered assets are only as good as their inputs, and in the absence of
a maintained description they will reconstruct one from whatever documents they
can reach — which means yesterday's assumptions, restated confidently.

It's also the least exciting part of the work and the part most likely to be
skipped, which is precisely why so many AI-in-marketing pilots produce impressive
demos and unusable output.
