If the machine can finally understand what you meant, a great deal of interface
design was solving a problem that is going away. The instinct is to conclude
"put a chat box on everything," which is the wrong lesson and an expensive one.

The real shift is in what the interface is *for*. It stops being the place you
perform operations and becomes the place you express an outcome, then supervise
its execution: steering when the system drifts, verifying what it produced,
deciding whether to trust it.

That reassigns the design work rather than removing it. The hard surfaces are now
correction (how do I redirect this without starting over?), legibility (what did
it actually do?) and calibration (how sure should I be?). Those are harder
problems than menu hierarchies, and most products haven't started on them.

Note the trade the shift makes: natural language is expressive but high-effort,
while a button is narrow and nearly free. Replacing every button with a sentence
taxes the user for flexibility they mostly didn't need.
