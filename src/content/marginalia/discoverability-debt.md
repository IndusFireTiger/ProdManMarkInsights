Menus, toolbars and navigation are usually described as ways to *operate*
software. They're also how software advertises what it can do. Every item is a
small claim about a capability that exists.

Natural-language interfaces discard that inventory. The user faces a blank field
and must supply not only the instruction but the *idea* — and people only ask for
what they already believe is possible. Capability that isn't imagined isn't
requested, and capability that isn't requested may as well not have shipped.

The debt compounds quietly. Usage concentrates on the handful of things early
users happened to try; those get optimised; the rest of the product becomes
progressively less discoverable relative to what the system can actually do. The
telemetry looks like focused engagement rather than a shrinking imagination of
the product.

Paying it down means reintroducing inventory without reintroducing the menu —
suggested actions, worked examples, visible affordances around the input. The old
interfaces solved a real problem, and only part of that problem was operation.
