## What it is

The maximum a buyer would pay before choosing their
[alternative](/marginalia/competitive-alternative). It's a property of the
*comparison*, not of your cost structure and not of your effort.

## Where people get it wrong

**Reading usage as value.** The most common and most expensive error. Frequency
is a behavioural signal; it often measures habit, or the absence of friction, or
the fact that something is free. The features people touch constantly are
regularly the ones they'd least pay for, because ubiquitous capabilities have
ubiquitous substitutes.

**Gating the habit loop.** Following the usage signal leads directly to
paywalling the most-used feature — which suppresses the top of the funnel and the
word-of-mouth surface to win a handful of conversions. The guardrail is simple:
never gate the daily loop; gate the outcome.

**Taking survey answers at face value.** Stated willingness to pay is notoriously
unreliable, and the people who answer pricing surveys are already engaged —
[selection bias](/marginalia/selection-bias) again. Revealed behaviour beats
stated intent.

**Mistaking a rationalised price for a derived one.** If a number arrives first
and the analysis follows, you've built a justification. Deriving WTP means
starting from the alternative and working forward.

## How to check

Two questions, in order: *what would you do instead?* and *what does that cost
you today?* The gap between those and your price is your actual pricing headroom.

Then check the seam: find where discounting spikes and where churn clusters.
Those thresholds are the market telling you where willingness genuinely ends.
