## What it is

The starting input of positioning, and the most commonly mis-specified one.
Alternatives are defined by the buyer's actual behaviour, not by your competitive
landscape slide — which means "an Excel sheet and a fortnightly meeting" is a
legitimate and frequently the *dominant* alternative.

## Where people get it wrong

**Listing vendors.** The competitor set in the deck is assembled from who you
lose deals to, which is a survivor-biased sample of deals that reached a
comparison at all. Most opportunities never get that far; they end in the status
quo.

**Ignoring the strongest alternative — inertia.** In enterprise sales the
decisive competitor is usually [the institutional
"no"](/marginalia/institutional-no): the option of continuing exactly as before,
which costs nothing to choose and requires no one to defend it.

**Anchoring price against the wrong thing.** Price expectations follow directly
from the alternative the buyer has in mind. Same product, same features, framed
against a spreadsheet or against a consultant, supports wildly different numbers.
Getting the alternative wrong makes every downstream pricing conversation wrong.

## How to check

Ask ten customers what they used before, in their words, and count how many name
a product at all. The share who describe a manual workaround is your real
alternative distribution.

For pricing, ask the follow-up: *what did that cost you — in money, or in hours?*
That figure, not your competitor's list price, is the anchor you're arguing
against.
