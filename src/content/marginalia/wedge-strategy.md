## What it is

A market-entry shape. Pick a segment narrow enough that you can be the obvious
choice, win it decisively, then use that proof and those relationships to
expand into adjacent segments. The wedge is chosen for the leverage it creates,
not merely because it's easy.

## Where people get it wrong

**Choosing a wedge with no expansion path.** A defensible niche that connects to
nothing is a lifestyle business, not a wedge. The test is whether winning it
makes the *next* segment cheaper to win — through reference customers, shared
workflows, or a regulatory approval that travels.

**Confusing narrow with small.** The wedge should be narrow in *definition* and
substantial in *value*. "Compliance reporting for mid-size Australian mutuals" is
narrow and lucrative; "small businesses" is broad and poor.

**Storming instead of entering.** Arriving in a fragmented market with trusted
incumbents and competing everywhere at once is the instinct of a strong product
and usually the wrong move. Entering as a layer — partnering, integrating,
supplying the engine beneath someone else's distribution — buys trust that can't
be built quickly from outside.

**Ignoring how the market is actually segmented.** Buyers are often divided by
funding line, jurisdiction or procurement regime rather than by size or industry.
The org chart of the market is the go-to-market plan.

## How to check

Name the wedge in one sentence a stranger could act on. If it takes a paragraph
and three qualifiers, it isn't a wedge — it's a target list.

Then ask what the second wedge is, and whether winning the first genuinely
helps. If it doesn't, you've picked a niche.
