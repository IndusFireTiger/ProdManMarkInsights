## What it is

Not copy, and not a tagline. Positioning sets the implicit assumptions a buyer
makes before you say anything: what kind of thing this is, what it should be
compared against, who it's for, and roughly what it ought to cost. April
Dunford's formulation builds it from four inputs — competitive alternatives,
unique attributes, the value those enable, and the customers who care most.

## Where people get it wrong

**Writing it after the build.** Positioning constructed post-hoc describes what
got made, because that's all that's left to describe. Done
[upstream](/marginalia/upstream-product-marketing), it's an input to scope
instead — which is the entire argument for moving it earlier.

**Claiming everything.** The strongest tell of real positioning is that it
*forecloses* options: it names a buyer you're not for and a comparison you'll
lose. A position that costs nothing to hold isn't one. This is also why
generative tools draft weak positioning by default — trained toward
helpfulness, they'll happily claim every segment.

**Confusing it with differentiation.** Differentiation is what's different about
you. Positioning is the *context* that makes the difference matter. A faster
database means nothing until the buyer has decided they're shopping for a
database and that speed is the axis.

## How to check

Ask what the position costs you. If you can't name the deal you'd walk away from
or the feature request you'd refuse, you have a description, not a position.

Then test it against the one comparison that decides most deals: not the rival
vendor, but doing nothing.
