Personalisation in education is usually pitched as removing obstacles: detect
where the learner is stuck and smooth the path. Half of that is right. Pace,
sequencing and framing should absolutely adapt. But the difficulty itself is
frequently the thing doing the teaching — effortful retrieval and productive
confusion are what convert exposure into understanding.

Which sets up the sharpest conflict in EdTech product design. The most *engaging*
product is the instant-answer machine: it maximises the feeling of progress,
scores brilliantly in week one, and is the one a student will rate highest. It
also short-circuits precisely the process that produces learning.

So the rule is a division: **adapt the pace, protect the struggle.** Adjust how
fast, how much scaffolding, what order, what encouragement. Don't adjust away the
part where the learner has to do the thinking.

It's a genuine [position](/marginalia/positioning) rather than a preference,
because it costs something — it means declining to build the more engaging
product, and holding that line when a customer asks for the answer machine
because their students prefer it.
