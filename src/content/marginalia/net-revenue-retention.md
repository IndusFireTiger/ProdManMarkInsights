## What it is

A cohort measure of whether your product becomes more valuable to customers over
time. Because it excludes new business, it isolates the health of what you've
already sold — which is why investors weight it so heavily and why it's hard to
flatter.

## Where people get it wrong

**Reading a high number as product health.** NRR can be held up by contractual
escalators, seat growth at customers who are themselves growing, or price rises
— none of which indicate anyone values the product more. Strip those out before
concluding anything.

**Letting the aggregate hide the segments.** A handful of large expanding
accounts routinely masks broad erosion in the mid-market. Segment it or it will
mislead you, cheerfully, for several quarters.

**Treating it as a customer-success metric.** By the time NRR moves, the causes
are a year old and sit in product and pricing decisions, not in renewal
conversations. It's a scoreboard, not a lever.

**Forgetting it only describes survivors.** NRR looks at customers who were there
at the start of the period. Everyone who churned before the window doesn't
appear — the same [survivorship blind spot](/marginalia/survivorship-bias) that
affects most retention analytics.

## How to check

Decompose it every time: expansion, contraction, churn, price. Four numbers, four
different stories, and only two of them are about the product being good.

Then read it beside [time to first value](/marginalia/time-to-first-value) for
recent cohorts. Healthy NRR with worsening TTFV means you're living on an
installed base you're no longer replicating.
